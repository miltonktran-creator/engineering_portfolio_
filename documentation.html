PROJECT / 01

FRC 9470 REEFSCAPE

2025 season · Two robot iterations

COMPETITION ROBOT
OVERVIEW

REEFSCAPE challenged teams to collect and score coral on a reef while moving algae to designated zones. Our robots focused on scoring across all reef levels with an elevator-mounted end effector, while also removing algae to create scoring opportunities for alliance partners.

Robot 1 · Pinnacles Regional

I led the robot layout and focused on the end effector, elevator, and drivetrain. WCP Swerve X2T modules helped preserve internal space for electronics and mechanisms. A belt-driven funnel guided coral into a horizontal roller end effector mounted on a two-stage cascade elevator, allowing the robot to reach all reef levels quickly.

During the event, we discovered that the algae intake was not needed for removing algae off the reef. Also the position of the elevator led to the suboptimal geometry for the end effector.

The robot ranked 15th, became the first pick of Alliance 3, advanced to the quarterfinals, and won the Autonomous Award. The event established a strong baseline for the next iteration.

Robot 2 · East Bay Regional

I helped refine the end effector into a much lighter mechanism using bottom rollers and a single motor, reducing its weight from seven pounds to just over two pounds. The elevator was moved more towards the front of the robot, in order to move the end effector closer to the scoring area. A gravity-fed funnel with 3D-printed TPU star wheels improved coral alignment, feeding reliability, and intake area while removing the need for a powered funnel. Instead of a bulky algae intake, I designed a mechanism that rotated two carbon fiber sticks that could easily remove the algae from the reef.

This robot ranked 16th and joined Alliance 6, reaching round 3 of playoffs.

Overall, this robot was a lighter, faster, and more optimized version of its predecessor, our Pinnacles robot and this time we were not 4 pounds overweight on load in day!

PROJECT / 02

FRC 9470 REBUILT

2026 season · Design, competition, rebuild

COMPETITION ROBOT
OVERVIEW

REBUILT centered on collecting and shooting fuel into alliance hubs. Our design priority was to maximize fuel throughput with a wide intake, fast internal transfer, and a high-output shooter.

Robot 1 · District Events

I designed the robot around a 27.5x27.5 inch drivetrain using WCP Swerve X2T modules, whose profiles created flexibility for more room for electronics and mechanisms. The 25-inch-wide slap down intake also extended the hopper capacity when deployed. An active hopper floor covered in 1.25-inch rollers moved fuel quickly toward the shooter. A simple three-lane shooter increased throughput, which we prioritized over accuracy so we could avoid the complexity of a turret. This simple, reliable robot allowed us to not only get selected to join Alliance 1, but also get our first ever event win.

For our next district event, we added an aluminum tube in front of our intake rollers in order to protect it from high force impacts. This made our intake much more reliable and allowed us to join Alliance 1 and secure our seccond event win.

Robot 2 · District Championship & Worlds

We rebuilt the robot from scratch on an SDS MK5n swerve base. I revised the intake for easier repair, strengthened the hopper walls, added a cieling to the hopper, and changed the floor to use compliant wheels in the center with lighter carbon-fiber rollers elsewhere. When the robot was disabled, the hopper floor could rotate upwards to improve access to electrical systems. Finally, the shooter was redesigned to a full-width drum to maximize output. Removing the need for lanes in the shooter greatly improved our throughput from nine balls per second to about 25 balls per second

District Championships exposed weaknesses in intake durability, trench clearance, and the shooter's hood rigidity, especially during playoffs. For Worlds, we rebuilt the intake with double-stacked 0.25-inch SRPP and added a bolt-and-bearing constraint to reduce hood vibration. The robot finished second in the Archimedes Division and joined Alliance 1 to win the division, then went 1–2 on Einstein Field.

PROJECT / 03

Custom CNC Router

Mechanical design · Electronics · Motion control

MACHINE DESIGN
OVERVIEW

This was my first project that combined mechanical design, electronics, and software into one machine. The goal was to build a computer-controlled router for cutting wood and plastic, as well as engraving small gifts and stationery.

Design & integration

The design drew inspiration from a similar machine on Youtube by Ivan Miranda but was built entirely from reused parts and 3D-printed components. The frame was constructed from 2020 aluminum extrusions for rigidity, while the motion system relied on lead screws and linear rails and belts I harvested from my old Flashforge Creator Pro printer. 

The CNC was controlled using 4 NEMA 17 stepper motors connected to their own motor microstepper drivers (the two Y axis motors were connected to the same driver but in reverse) and an Arduino UNO R3 running GRBL, which ran the Gcode for the machine. All the electronics were powered with a Meanwell LRS-350-24 power supply which could supply 24V at 14.6 A. I used the open-source Universal Gcode Sender to handle the calibration, homing and other general CNC controls. Since I had access to Fusion 360, I used the CAM software that comes with the student license to convert my 3D models into toolpaths. 

The spindle motor of this machine was my readily available corded Dewalt handheld router meant for woodworking. Because the goal of this project was to cut wood and plastic, I did not need an expensive spindle with precise controls.

Challenges & lessons

After the first cutting run, chips and dust accumulated around the belts and lead screws. The added resistance exceeded what the NEMA 17 motors could reliably overcome. This revealed the importance of protecting motion components from debris; a spindle dust shield that redirects chips away from the drive system is definetly as a key improvement for my next iteration.

PROJECT / 04

Two-Axis Gimbal

Gearbox Design · Inverse Kinematics · Motor Control

ROBOTICS / MECHANISMS
OVERVIEW

I developed a two-axis robotic mechanism that positions an end effector in a 2D plane using two motors and interconnected arms arranged as a parallelogram linkage.

Cycloidal gearbox iteration

The first NEMA 17 cycloidal drive used a 15:1 reduction ratio and a footprint matching the motor. I used this prototype to test basic control code, accuracy, and repeatability. Testing showed that the discs could wobble inside the gearbox, and the M3 washers I added to separate them as a quick fix created unwanted friction.

For the next iteration, I added grooves to the discs to hold bearing balls. The balls moved with the discs, reducing wobble while allowing smoother motion. The final drive used a 26:1 reduction ratio in a 70 mm diameter footprint, with the output moved to the outer drum. The design changes came directly from testing the first prototype and addressing its observed limitations. The main benefit of moving the output was the flexibility when mounting robotic components. Instead of needing a bolt pattern to mount parts, the cycloidal lobe pattern could be integrated directly into the part, or a spline pattern could be created on the outer drum. 

Arm mechanism & kinematics

I stacked two finalized cycloidal drives and attached a custom two-link arm to each outer-drum output. Connecting the arms created a parallelogram linkage for coordinated planar motion. I also derived inverse-kinematics equations by hand to calculate the motor angles needed to position the end effector at a target (x,y) coordinate.
